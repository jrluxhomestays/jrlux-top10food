Top 10 Malaysian Foods

The Dishes Every Guest Should Try

Ten much-loved dishes, with their story, community, records and a price guide. Tap a dish to open it.

Halal note: Bak Kut Teh is non-halal. Most other dishes are sold halal in malls and restaurants, but we do not certify any outlet, so look for the halal logo or ask the staff. Ingredients are typical and recipes vary, so please tell the staff about any allergies (peanuts, shrimp paste, shellfish and gluten are common). Prices are rough guides only and vary by city, location and stall.
1Nasi LemakHalal widely available

CommunityMalay

StoryFragrant coconut rice served with sambal, anchovies, peanuts and egg. Its first mention in print dates to 1909, in a description of Malay weddings and festivals. A popular legend tells of a Malacca woman who spilled coconut milk into her rice. Her daughter said "nasi le, mak" ("just rice, Mum"), and the dish took its name.

Key ingredientsRice cooked in coconut milk with pandan leaf, served with sambal (chilli paste), fried anchovies, roasted peanuts, cucumber and egg. Often with fried chicken or rendang.

Why it's lovedRegarded as Malaysia's national dish, and a breakfast staple wrapped in banana leaf and eaten at any hour.

RecordRecognised by Malaysia's National Heritage Department on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

Price guide (varies by location)Plain packet about RM3.50–3.80. With extras such as fried chicken, about RM7–12. In the past it sold for as little as 30 sen.

2Roti CanaiHalal widely available

CommunityIndian Muslim (mamak)

StoryA flaky, buttery flatbread tossed and folded by hand and served with dhal or curry. It descends from the Indian paratha, brought by Indian immigrants in the colonial era and spread across the country by Indian Muslim mamak stalls. The origin of the word "canai" is debated: some link it to Chennai, others to a Malay word meaning to knead.

Key ingredientsWheat flour, water, salt, sugar and ghee or oil. Served with dhal or curry, and sometimes egg.

Why it's lovedEaten by every community, from breakfast to supper.

RecordReported to have been recognised as part of Malaysia's breakfast culture on UNESCO's intangible heritage list.

Price guide (varies by location)Plain about RM1.50–2.50. With egg or fillings, about RM2.50–5.

3Penang Assam LaksaHalal widely available

CommunityPeranakan (Straits Chinese)

StoryThick rice noodles in a sour, spicy tamarind and mackerel broth with mint, pineapple and shrimp paste. The dish grew out of Penang's days as a spice-route trading port and the blending of Chinese and Malay kitchens through Peranakan families.

Key ingredientsThick rice noodles in a broth of mackerel, tamarind (asam), lemongrass, galangal and chilli. Topped with cucumber, pineapple, onion, mint, torch ginger flower and shrimp paste (hae ko).

Why it's lovedPenang's signature bowl, and a favourite for visitors who come to the island for food.

RecordRanked No. 7 on CNN's World's 50 Best Foods list (2020, as reported).

Price guide (varies by location)About RM6–12 at hawker stalls and kopitiams. Tourist-area stalls can charge much more.

4Kajang SatayHalal widely available

CommunityJavanese-Malay

StoryCharcoal-grilled skewers with a sweet, rich peanut sauce, served with ketupat, onion and cucumber. Javanese settlers brought satay to Kajang, and Haji Tasmin and his brother Rono are credited with popularising it there in the 1910s. Haji Samuri began his stall in 1992 and grew it to about 20 outlets in Selangor, Kuala Lumpur and Negeri Sembilan.

Key ingredientsMarinated chicken or beef skewers grilled over charcoal. Marinade of turmeric, lemongrass, galangal and spices. Served with peanut sauce, ketupat (rice cake), onion and cucumber.

Why it's lovedKajang is known as Malaysia's satay town, and Sate Kajang Haji Samuri is its best-known name.

RecordNamed Best Satay Eatery in the Klang Valley at The Star People's Food Awards (2014).

Price guide (varies by location)Reports from 2013–2018 put chicken satay at about RM0.70–0.98 a stick. Today's prices are higher, so please check at the stall.

5Hokkien Mee (KL and Penang)NON-HALAL (traditionally uses pork lard)

CommunityHokkien Chinese

StoryTwo famous dishes share one name. Kuala Lumpur's version has thick noodles fried over charcoal in dark soy, traditionally with pork lard. Kim Lian Kee, founded in 1927 by Ong Kim Lian, is known as its birthplace. Penang's version is a spicy prawn soup made from fried prawn heads and shells. Elsewhere the Penang dish is often called prawn mee.

Key ingredientsKL: thick yellow noodles, dark soy sauce, pork, pork lard, squid and greens. Penang: egg and rice noodles in a prawn and pork-bone broth with prawns, kangkung, egg and chilli paste.

Why it's lovedA KL institution for nearly a century, and one of Penang's must-try hawker bowls.

RecordKim Lian Kee has served KL Hokkien mee since 1927.

Price guide (varies by location)Prices vary by outlet. Please check the menu.

6Hainanese Chicken RiceHalal widely available

CommunityHainanese Chinese

StoryPoached chicken served with rice cooked in chicken stock, with chilli and ginger sauces. Hainanese immigrants adapted a Hainan dish for Southeast Asia. In Malaysia, one account has Liang Juqing learning it in Malacca in the 1920s and opening a Klang stall in the 1930s, and Nam Heong dates its shop to 1939. Malaysia and Singapore both claim the dish.

Key ingredientsPoached chicken, rice cooked in chicken stock with ginger, garlic and pandan. Served with chilli, ginger and dark soy sauces, and cucumber.

Why it's lovedEveryday comfort food, especially loved in Ipoh and Kuala Lumpur.

RecordNam Heong Chicken Rice is a MICHELIN Bib Gourmand pick in Kuala Lumpur.

Price guide (varies by location)About RM7–14 a plate: roughly RM8–12 at hawker centres and RM12–18 at food courts.

7Bak Kut TehNON-HALAL (contains pork)

CommunityHokkien and Teochew Chinese

StoryPork ribs simmered for hours in a peppery, herbal broth. Klang is widely seen as its home. One story links it to dockworkers at Port Swettenham (now Port Klang) in the early 1900s, and restaurants began selling it around the 1940s. The "teh" means herbal tea. Singapore also claims the dish, so its exact origin is debated.

Key ingredientsPork ribs simmered with garlic, white pepper and Chinese herbs such as angelica root, star anise, cinnamon and cloves. Served with rice, you tiao (fried dough) and tea.

Why it's lovedA Klang pride and a weekend family ritual across the Klang Valley.

RecordThree Kuala Lumpur bak kut teh restaurants are MICHELIN Bib Gourmand picks in 2027: Ah Hei, Hing Kee and Minji.

Price guide (varies by location)A set for one starts at about RM18 excluding rice and drinks (2025 report), compared with about RM2 in the 1970s.

8Ramly BurgerHalal widely available

CommunityMalay

StoryA street burger wrapped in egg, with a beef or chicken patty, onion, sauces and margarine. Ramly Mokni and his wife Shala Siah started in 1978–79 in Kuala Lumpur with about 200 patties a day. He is said to have begun after working as a butcher and wanting a halal product.

Key ingredientsBeef or chicken patty wrapped in a fried egg, with onion, cabbage, margarine, chilli and mayonnaise sauces in a soft bun.

Why it's lovedThe pasar malam burger, sold by thousands of small stall owners.

RecordReported annual sales of about RM1 billion around 2019, with about 70% local and 30% exported. Output has grown to about a million patties a day (reported).

Price guide (varies by location)Prices vary by stall.

9Curry Puff (Karipap)Halal widely available

CommunityMalay, Chinese and Indian

StoryA crisp pastry filled with curried potato, chicken or sardine. Its origin is unclear. Popular theories point to the Portuguese empanada, the Cornish pasty or the Indian samosa, and by the early 1900s it was a common market snack in Malaya and Singapore.

Key ingredientsFlaky or crisp pastry filled with curried potato, onion and chicken or sardine, sometimes with boiled egg.

Why it's lovedSold at kopitiams, bakeries and bazaars, and a favourite with tea.

RecordA hybrid that every Malaysian community claims as its own.

Price guide (varies by location)About RM2.80 each at OldTown White Coffee (listing: RM5.60 for two).

10RojakHalal widely available

CommunityJavanese origin · Chinese and Indian Muslim variants

Story"Rojak" means "mixed". Fruit rojak combines cucumber, pineapple, jicama, beansprouts and fried dough in a sweet, tangy prawn-paste sauce. Penang's version often adds water apple and guava. Penang's Indian rojak, pasembur, was created by Indian Muslim hawkers in the 20th century.

Key ingredientsFruit rojak: cucumber, pineapple, jicama (bengkuang), green mango, beansprouts and fried dough, in a sauce of shrimp paste, tamarind, sugar and chilli, topped with crushed peanuts.

Why it's lovedA Penang favourite, and a sweet-sour-spicy snack in the heat.

RecordRojak in Malaysian slang also means a mixed-up bunch, which suits a multicultural country.

Price guide (varies by location)Prices vary by stall.

Prices are indicative only. Actual prices vary by city, area, mall or hawker stall, and change over time.
